# Priority Guidelines

Priority is a response signal, not a measure of frustration.

| Priority | Use when | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | A significant production incident or critical operational failure is active | Start the established incident response immediately |
| High | A serious degradation or significant delivery blocker needs coordinated attention | Coordinate promptly within the required delivery window |
| Normal | A routine defect, integration problem, or non-production issue can follow normal workflow | Handle during business hours |
| Low | A minor defect, improvement, or non-urgent request can be prioritised later | Add to backlog planning |

## Environment is context, not severity

Production does not automatically mean Critical, and non-production does not automatically mean Normal. A failed disaster-recovery exercise, blocked regulatory validation, or imminent release failure may justify High or Critical treatment outside production.

## Update priority as facts change

Priority is not permanent. Increase it when scope or impact grows; reduce it when evidence shows the effect is contained. State the reason for a meaningful change in the existing incident or ticket record.

## Security

A suspected security incident must be reported through the approved security process immediately. Do not wait for production impact to be confirmed, and do not place sensitive evidence in a public or broadly accessible message.

## Local mappings

Organisations should publish a short mapping from S.P.I.N. priorities to their established severity levels, response targets, and on-call rules. The local policy remains authoritative.
